Scout Lens

Scout's C# rules already find the pauses. They can't weigh the small things you see on the chart: a pause sitting under last week's high, a move that has run too far, a messy pause. Scout Lens is a layer that sits after the detector and decides, for each setup the rules find, whether to take it, skip it, or trade it smaller. This page lays out five ways to build it, with two chart examples for each.

The rules stay the safety net. The layer can only remove or shrink an order the rules already wanted. It never invents one.

Option 1

Setup scorer

A small machine-learning model gives every setup a score: the profit it expects in R. Scout takes high scores, shrinks middling ones and skips low ones.

Strong fit · recommended

Each candidate becomes one row of numbers. Most are already in setups.csv: pause candles, pause height and move in ATR, pull-back, ATR, and whether it agrees with the 4H trend. We add context the rules don't use: hour and session, how many steps into the staircase this pause is, distance to the last swing high or low, spread, and recent volatility. A gradient-boosted tree model (LightGBM) is trained in Python on years of replayed setups, saved as an ONNX file, and loaded by Trader.Scout through ONNX Runtime. Scoring takes under a millisecond.

Data to start
10,000+ labelled setups from replaying years of 1H history
Runs
Inside Trader.Scout (C#), offline
Effort
Medium. The replay simulator is the big piece.
Main risk
Overfitting; future data leaking into training

+ Pros

  • The standard, proven approach for filtering signals from a rule-based system.
  • Works with thousands of examples, not millions.
  • You can see which inputs pushed each score up or down.
  • Same answer every time; fully backtestable over years.
  • No outside service; runs in the bot.

− Cons

  • Needs a replay simulator that plays out fills, stop-loss and trailing before any training.
  • Learns only caveats you can turn into a number.
  • Needs retraining as markets change.
  • The live trial (a few hundred setups so far) is far too small to train on alone.
Option 2

Learn your eye

You mark candidates take or skip, with a quick reason. A model learns to predict your call.

Supporting role

Scout's candidates, live or from replayed history, appear on a small labelling screen with the order picture. You tap Take or Skip and pick a reason. Your labels train a classifier on the same feature rows as option 1, or on the chart pictures themselves. Your reasons also become a written list of caveats that options 1 and 3 can use.

Data to start
1,000–3,000 of your labels
Runs
Like option 1 once trained
Effort
Medium–high: a labelling screen plus your time
Main risk
Copies your habits, the bad ones too

+ Pros

  • Captures the context you see and the rules miss, the "not mathematical" part.
  • Turns your judgment into a written caveat list.
  • Keeps you in the loop while Scout learns.

− Cons

  • Takes a lot of your time, about 15 minutes a day for months.
  • Learns what you would take, not what pays. It must be checked against real outcomes.
  • A model that reads chart pictures needs far more labels than one person can make.
  • Your eye shifts over time, so early and late labels disagree.
Option 3

Claude chart reviewer

When a setup fires, Scout shows Claude the chart and your caveat list. Claude answers approve, smaller size, or veto, with a reason.

Strong fit · recommended, shadow mode first

On each NewStop or NewReversal, Scout sends the order picture it already draws (converted to PNG), the setup row, the last 100 candles of 1H and 4H, and your caveats written in plain English. Claude replies in a fixed JSON shape. Fixed rules around it: Claude can only veto or shrink; if it is slow or down, Scout falls back to the rules alone; the model version is pinned; every prompt and reply is logged.

Data to start
None. Your caveat list, in words.
Runs
API call from Scout, a few seconds; fine on 1H
Effort
Low–medium
Main risk
Inconsistent answers; outside dependency

+ Pros

  • No training. Write a new caveat today and it applies at the next candle.
  • Gives a reason for every decision, in words you can check.
  • Handles fuzzy context: nearby levels, stretched moves, messy pauses.
  • Reads the same picture you look at.

− Cons

  • Its "confidence" is not a real probability.
  • Can answer differently on the same setup.
  • Slow and costly to backtest over years of setups.
  • Depends on an outside API; model updates can change its behaviour.
  • Can sound convincing while wrong, so shadow mode comes first.
Option 4

Auto-tune the thresholds

Search for the rule settings (pause length, maximum pause height, minimum move) that made the most R in the past.

Side tool only

An optimiser tries thousands of setting combinations against replayed history and keeps the best set, which then goes into scout.json. Nothing new runs inside Scout. Useful as a check that today's settings are reasonable, not as the AI layer.

Data to start
Replay results (same as option 1)
Runs
Offline; Scout just gets new numbers
Effort
Low once replay exists
Main risk
High overfitting

+ Pros

  • Cheapest option.
  • Rules stay readable; no new moving parts in the bot.
  • Shows how sensitive the setup is to each setting.

− Cons

  • The easiest way to fit the past and fail live.
  • Adds no new context; it only moves the same lines.
  • The best settings drift as markets change.
Option 5

End-to-end deep learning

A neural network (or reinforcement learning) reads raw candles and decides buy or sell by itself, with no pause rules.

Not recommended

The network replaces the detector instead of helping it. It needs huge amounts of data and GPU training, then runs through ONNX like option 1.

Data to start
Hundreds of thousands of examples or more
Runs
GPU training; ONNX in Scout
Effort
High
Main risk
Learns noise; can't explain itself

+ Pros

  • Could in theory find patterns nobody wrote down.
  • No hand-made inputs needed.

− Cons

  • Throws away the setup you have spent weeks teaching Scout.
  • Needs far more data than 1H forex and crypto history provide.
  • Fits noise easily; reinforcement learning on markets is unstable.
  • Gives no reason for any trade.

Recommendation

Options 1 and 3 together, in shadow mode first

The scorer handles what can be measured; the reviewer handles what can only be described. Every time you think "the rules miss X", X becomes either a new scorer input (if it can be measured) or a new line in the reviewer's caveat list (if it can't).

  1. Build the dataset. Add an outcome simulator to --replay that plays every historical setup forward with Scout's real order rules: stop entry, stop-loss at the far side of the pause, trailing, 16-candle expiry, cancel when the setup ends. Run it on several years of 1H candles across 20+ symbols. Each setup gets its result in R.
  2. Baseline. Before any ML, slice the results by the existing fields: with or against 4H, pause height, staircase step, session. One plain filter is often most of the gain. The trial review due 10 Nov feeds straight into this.
  3. Train the scorer. Walk-forward, with a gap between training and test periods so overlapping staircase setups don't leak. Judge it by total R and R per trade, not win rate.
  4. Add the reviewer. Write your caveat list; wire the Claude call with the fixed reply shape, veto-only rights and a fallback.
  5. Shadow mode, 2–4 weeks. Both write their score or verdict to a new ai.csv, linked by order id, but change nothing. Afterwards, compare the setups they would have skipped with what really happened.
  6. Switch on. Only if the skipped setups really did worse, and total R goes up, not just win rate.

Two warnings. Filtering changes the strategy: you take every pause on purpose and accept the losers, so a filter that cuts losers will also cut some big staircase winners. And every input must be known when the candle closes; the 4H trend tag and the pause edges are where future data most often slips in.
